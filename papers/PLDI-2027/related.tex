\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Denotational compiler correctness}

The idea that a compiler is correct when a diagram of meanings commutes
goes back to \citet{Morris:1973ab} and \citet{Thatcher:1981aa}, and
\citet{Milne:1976aa} related semantics at different levels of
abstraction with inclusive predicates. Early mechanized compiler
proofs were operational: the CLI stack in Nqthm
\citep{Moore:1989aa,Young:1989aa} runs the source for $n$ steps and the
target for a number of steps given by a clock function, and shows that
runs without error are preserved, but says nothing about divergence.
Among mechanized denotational proofs,
\citet{Benton:2009aa} relate a domain-theoretic semantics of a typed
language with recursion to an operational SECD machine, using
step-indexed biorthogonal relations; the target machine has primitive
closures. \citet{Pardo:2018aa} give clocked denotations to a
first-order imperative language and a stack language in Agda, and
\OE{}uf \citep{Mullen:2018aa} gives a denotation only to its total
source language and proves every pass operationally. The calculated
compilers of \citet{Bahr:2015aa} derive a compiler and its virtual
machine from a definitional interpreter; the virtual machine keeps
closures as values. \citet{Siek:2017ab} proves an inliner correct by
equality of graph-model denotations, and \citet{Siek:2025ab} proves the
back-end passes of a first-order compiler correct operationally; our
back end adapts the latter.

\paragraph{Closure conversion}

Typed closure conversion represents a closure as a package of an
existential type \citep{Minamide:1996ys}, as our FlatClos does, and is
proved correct with logical relations over terms. \citet{Ahmed:2008aa}
show that it preserves observational equivalence, and
\citet{Bowman:2018aa} extend it to the calculus of constructions. \citet{Wand:1994aa} prove the partial
correctness of lightweight closure conversion with an operational
semantics. Mechanized proofs of closure conversion for
languages with recursion, such as those of \citet{Hur:2011aa},
\citet{Neis:2015aa}, and \citet{Wang:2016ab}, use Kripke, step-indexed,
or simulation relations over operational semantics.

\paragraph{Relations between denotations}

Relating two denotational semantics of one language is an old problem.
The tower $\ALR_0, \ALR_1, \ldots$ of the approximant logical relation
(Section~\ref{sec:approx-lr}) has the same shape as the one that
\citet{Reynolds:1974aa} used to relate the direct and continuation
semantics of a language. He built his relation stage by stage along the
inverse-limit construction of the domains
\citep{Scott:1971aa,Smyth:1982aa}. His index ranges over the stages of
both domains, and his relation is the intersection over all stages.
Our index bounds only the depth of the finite target side, and depth
irrelevance (Lemma~\ref{lem:depth-irrelevant}) makes an intersection
unnecessary. Later work related two semantics with inclusive predicates
\citep{Milne:1976aa,Stoy:1977aa}, finitary projections
\citep{Mulmuley:1987aa}, or types \citep{Meyer:1985aa,Filinski:2007aa}.
\citet{Pitts:1996aa} showed that a relation specified by a
mixed-variance equation over a recursively defined domain exists and
is unique, because the domain is the minimal invariant of its defining
functor. In our Scott-domain proof of the same compiler
(Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}), his method did not apply directly to
\pass{delay}, and we built the relations stage by stage instead, as
Reynolds did. Indexing a relation by
finite elements is common in adequacy proofs, which relate a
denotation to syntax: \citet{Winskel:1993aa} indexes by the size of
tokens of an information system, \citet{Abramsky:1990vv} by formulas of
a domain logic, and \citet{Siek:2017ab} and \citet{Wadler:2020aa} by
finite elements of a graph or filter model. Step-indexed
\citep{Appel:2001aa,Ahmed:2006aa} and guarded
\citep{Mogelberg:2016aa} relations are the operational and synthetic
alternatives. To our knowledge, the approximant logical relation is
the first relation between two denotational semantics indexed by
finite approximations on one side only.

\paragraph{Graph models and their mechanization}

Our model descends from the graph models of \citet{Plotkin:1993ab},
\citet{Scott:1976lq}, and \citet{Engeler:1981aa} and is related to the
filter models of \citet{Barendregt:1983aa}. Consistency has a
precedent in the $\lambda$-calculus model that \citet{Scott:1982aa}
builds from information systems, whose function entries pair
consistent sets of inputs and outputs. As in the coherent domains of
\citet{Plotkin:1978aa}, our consistency is pairwise, and our rule for
entries is the function-space consistency of \citet{Larsen:1984aa} in
its pairwise form \citep{Schwichtenberg:2006aa}. 

\paragraph{Comparisons of semantic styles}

Besides the comparisons of \citet{Owens:2016aa} and
\citet{Kahn:2025aa} discussed in the introduction,
\citet{Leroy:2009rc} and \citet{Danielsson:2012aa} prove compilers
correct with coinductive big-step semantics and with the partiality
monad, and \citet{Knothe:2026aa} combine small-step and big-step
semantics within CompCert. None of these includes a
denotational semantics or a whole compiler for a higher-order
language.
