\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

This paper furthers our understanding of how the choice of language
semantics impacts proofs of compiler correctness and delivers two
novel denotational proofs of correctness for closure conversion. But before
explaining our results, let us review the state of the art. Thanks to
major compiler verification projects such as
CompCert~\citep{Leroy:2009aa} and
CakeML~\citep{Kumar:2014aa,Owens:2016aa} we have a fairly good
understanding of the use of operational semantics in proofs of
compiler correctness. CompCert~\citep{Leroy:2016aa} employs small-step
semantics and relates them using simulations. CakeML uses interpreters
and reasons about them using induction on evaluation and value
relations.  Both proofs were large undertakings. The proof of CompCert
is 100,000 lines of Coq and took 6 person-years \citep{Leroy:2016aa},
and the proof of the CakeML compiler backend is about 100,000 lines of
HOL4 \citep{Tan:2019aa}, by six people over two years
\citep{Myreen:2016aa}.

In contrast, the literature is less complete when it comes to the use
of denotational semantics in compiler correctness proofs, but there is
evidence that denotational semantics can yield simpler proofs due to
equational and compositional reasoning
\citep{Morris:1973ab,Stoy:1977aa,Zakowski:2021aa}. This
paper fills a fundamental gap in the literature, establishing the
first proofs of correctness with respect to denotational semantics
for a compiler from a Turing-complete, higher-order language to a
first-order language. Why is there such a fundamental gap in the
literature? We suspect two reasons: (1) the denotational semantics for
Turing-complete languages are more complex than those for
strongly-normalizing languages, and (2) compilers for higher-order
languages that target a first-order language must go through closure
conversion, which involves a change of representation, and that is
more challenging to reason about in denotational semantics. Recall
that \emph{closure conversion} replaces each function with a pair of
code and the values of its free variables, and each function call with
code that takes the pair
apart~\citep{Guy-L.-Steele:1978yq,Cardelli:1984aa}.

To date, every proof of compiler correctness with respect to a
denotational semantics stops short of closure conversion for a
Turing-complete language, in several different ways.
\citet{Nielsen:2000aa} proved that defunctionalization, a close cousin
of closure conversion, preserves the denotations of programs. However,
compiler correctness requires that the denotation of programs is also
reflected. Otherwise a compiler could translate a
diverging source program into a converging target.
\citet{Nielsen:2000aa} says ``Eventually, we want to prove full
correctness, i.e., semantic equivalence of the two programs.''
%
\citet{Banerjee:2001aa} proved both directions, but gave an
operational semantics to the source language. They report that they
``know of no other proofs of correctness (that is, both directions)
for ordinary or CFA-based defunctionalization in the presence of
recursion.''
%
\citet{Chlipala:2007fk} proved correctness of a compiler from the
simply typed $\lambda$-calculus (STLC) to assembly, including closure
conversion, but the STLC is not Turing-complete, which let him
interpret each type as a Coq type and each function as a total Coq
function.
%
Other denotational proofs of compiler correctness avoid closure
conversion, because either their source language is first-order
\citep{Polak:1981aa,Xia:2019aa,Cheng:2026aa} or their target language
is still higher-order \citep{Clinger:1984aa,Benton:2009aa}. When
\citet{Chlipala:2010aa} proved correct a compiler for a language with
general recursion, he switched to operational semantics, explaining
that the denotational approach ``cannot be applied directly to
Turing-complete programming languages.''

A natural setting to study the compilation of higher-order languages
is Plotkin's FPC language \citep{Plotkin:1985aa}, a call-by-value
$\lambda$-calculus with sums, products, and iso-recursive types. The
recursive types of FPC enable the fixed-point combinator, so \fpc{}
has general recursion and is Turing-complete.  That being said, FPC
lacks many of the features of modern programming languages, such as
mutable state, exceptions, and multi-threading, to name a few. The
claims in this paper are specific to the compilation of FPC. We make
no claims about the impact of semantic styles on proofs of correctness
for compilers of other programming languages.

The compiler for FPC that we study begins with a source optimization
pass based on the cp0 pass of Chez Scheme
\citep{Waddell:1997fk,Dybvig:2006aa}, includes three passes for
closure conversion, and four back-end passes that target a typed
low-level intermediate language. For this compiler we seek to answer
three research questions:
\begin{description}
\item[RQ1] Can we prove correctness of this compiler with respect to a
  denotational semantics?
\item[RQ2] How do the sizes of mechanized correctness proofs for this
  compiler compare across different operational and denotational
  semantic styles, pass by pass and overall?
\item[RQ3] Which properties of a pass determine the size of
  its correctness proof in each style?
\end{description}

We answer RQ1 affirmatively, in two denotational models. Our first
model is a graph model, in which a program denotes a set of
inductively defined graph
elements~\citep{Scott:1976lq,Engeler:1981aa,Plotkin:1993ab}. Each
element is a finite description of the program's behavior, and in
particular, the semantics of a function is given by a set of elements
that represent input--output entries.  To prove semantic reflection we
introduce a new kind of relation between denotations, the
\emph{approximant logical relation}. Our key insight is to relate a
finite set of target graph elements (a finite approximation) to a
possibly infinite source denotation, and to define the relation by
recursion on the depth of the elements in the finite set
(Section~\ref{sec:approx-lr}). Our second model uses Scott domains, in
which a program denotes an element of a universal domain of tagged
values~\citep{Scott:1982aa}.  There we define the relation by
recursion on the stages of the bilimit that constructs the domain
(Section~\ref{sec:scott-delay}), as \citet{Reynolds:1974aa} did to
relate the direct and continuation semantics of an untyped
$\lambda$-calculus. Other styles of denotational semantics
remain to be evaluated for compiler correctness and closure
conversion, among them synthetic
domain theory \citep{Rosolini:1986aa,Hyland:1991aa} and its guarded
variant \citep{Birkedal:2011aa,Mogelberg:2016aa}, guarded interaction
trees \citep{Frumin:2024aa}, categorical semantics
\citep{Fiore:1994aa}, the partiality monad \citep{Danielsson:2012aa},
and game semantics \citep{Hyland:2000ve}.

On top of our denotational results for closure conversion, we develop
two correctness proofs for the eight-pass compiler from \fpc{} to a
typed low-level intermediate language. For seven of the eight passes, correctness is
expressed as equality of denotations and the proofs are by induction
on terms and use equational reasoning.  The exception is the pass that
separates the code of each closure from its environment, \pass{split
  closure}, whose reflection proof uses the approximant logical
relation for the graph model and the relation on stages of the bilimit
for Scott domains.

To answer RQ2 and RQ3, we compare the two denotational proofs with
proofs of correctness of the same compiler with respect to three
operational semantics, each proved using the technique usual for its
style: clocked interpreters with relations on values, abstract
machines with simulations, and reduction semantics with simulations.
Earlier work that compares
proofs of compiler correctness focuses on a single phase or pass of a
compiler \citep{Owens:2016aa,Kahn:2025aa,Knothe:2026aa}; ours is the
first on a whole compiler for a higher-order language.
%
Each of the five correctness theorems is with respect to a different
semantics, so one might wonder whether the semantics and therefore the
theorems are equivalent.  To address this concern, we prove that the denotational semantics
of the source language \fpc\ and of the target language \instr\ are
adequate with respect to their interpreters
(Section~\ref{sec:adequacy}). We do not prove our interpreters
equivalent to our abstract machines and reduction semantics, but such
equivalences are well established for the $\lambda$-calculus
\citep{G.-D.-Plotkin:1975on,Felleisen:kx,Ager:2003fk}, and their
proofs would adapt readily to \fpc\ and \instr.

No one semantic style gives the smallest proof for every pass
(RQ2), but for every pass, the smallest proof is either the
graph-model proof or the interpreter proof. Counting each correctness proof together with the semantics of
its languages, but not counting language-independent infrastructure,
the clocked interpreters give the smallest proof, at 1,504 lines of
Agda, and the graph model gives the second smallest, at 1,808, ahead
of abstract machines, Scott domains, and reduction semantics. Two
factors largely explain the sizes (RQ3). The size of a denotational
proof tends to depend most on whether the pass preserves the meaning
of a program exactly. The source optimizer, \pass{simplify}, does, and
its graph-model and Scott-domain proofs take 349 and 411 lines,
against 508 to 697 for the operational proofs
(Section~\ref{sec:equational}).  \pass{split closure} does not, and
its proofs take 450 and 723 lines in the graph model and in Scott domains,
against 35 to 126 operationally. The size of an operational proof
tends to depend instead on how closely the run of the target follows
the run of the source.  The runs correspond closely for \pass{split
  closure}, which is why its operational proofs are small, and differ
more for \pass{simplify}, which inlines functions. Without \pass{split closure}, the graph-model proof would be
the smallest of the five. The graph-model proofs are smaller than the
Scott-domain proofs for all of the compiler passes
(Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}).

The rest of the paper is organized as follows.
Section~\ref{sec:compiler} presents the compiler and its eight
passes. Section~\ref{sec:graph-models} provides background on the
graph model, and Section~\ref{sec:compiler-proof} describes the proof
of correctness of each pass of the compiler with respect to the graph
model.  In addition, Section~\ref{sec:scott-delay} describes the proof
of correctness for \pass{split closure} with respect to Scott domains.
Section~\ref{sec:adequacy} proves the graph model adequate with
respect to the interpreters.
Section~\ref{sec:evaluation} compares the five proofs of correctness
and identifies which properties of the passes contribute to the sizes
of the proofs in the different semantic styles.
Section~\ref{sec:related} discusses related work, and
Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes. The appendices define the
semantics of every language and define every pass in the compiler.  We
mechanize all of our results in Agda and provide the development as
supplementary material.
