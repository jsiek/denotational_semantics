
When setting out to prove the correctness of a compiler, and important
question is: correctness with respect to which style of semantics?
The CakeML and CompCert projects, among others, have shown that
operational semantics such as abstract machines and interpreters are
viable choices. Their correctness proofs reason about program
execution using simulations and step-indexed logical relations. In
this paper we explore whether denotational semantics can also be used
in proofs of compiler correctness and evaluate the tradeoffs. Our
literature review revealed an important gap: there were no proofs of
compiler correctness with respect to denotational semantics for a
Turing-complete source language with lexically scoped functions,
through closure conversion, to a lower-level intermediate
language. Compiler correctness involves two directions: semantic
preservation and reflection, and for closure conversion, semantic
reflection is the hard part using denotational semantics. In this
paper we present the first two denotational proofs of semantic
reflection for closure conversion, one proof with respect to a graph
model and one proof with respect to a Scott domain. We developed the
new \emph{approximant logical relation} for the proof with the graph
model, and we adapted a logical relation of Reynolds for the proof
with the Scott domain.

As a first step in evaluating the tradeoffs between different
semantics, we conduct an apples-to-apples comparison between five
semantic styles in proving the correctness of a compiler from a typed
lambda calculus with recursive types down to a typed low-level
intermediate language. The five styles include the two denotational
semantics (graph model and Scott domain) and three operational
semantics: interpreters, reduction semantics, and abstract machines
(CEK). We discover that no one semantic style yields the shortest
proofs for every compiler pass and we isolate two factors that
influence proof size. For operational semantics, the proof size tends
to be proportional to how different the execution traces are for the
source and target programs of a compiler pass. For denotational
semantics, the proofs are succinct when the correctness of a pass can
be expressed as semantic equality, such as function inlining, but the
proofs become lengthy when a logical relation is needed for a pass,
such as closure conversion.
