Denotational semantics promises compositional, equational reasoning
about programs, and compiler correctness was among its first
applications, yet verified compilers today are almost entirely
operational. The denotational compiler proofs in the literature, on
paper or mechanized, either (1) are limited to source languages that
are first-order or strongly normalizing, or (2) compile to target
languages that treat closures as primitive values and so avoid closure
conversion. We use a denotational semantics based on a graph model to
prove a ten-pass compiler correct, from a call-by-value
$\lambda$-calculus with iso-recursive types (so it supports general
recursion and admits divergence), through closure conversion, to a
typed assembly language.  The proof is mechanized in Agda. Every
intermediate language has a denotational semantics, and most passes
are proved by an equation between the denotations of a program before
and after the pass. The exception is the step of closure conversion
that lowers closures into code and environment pairs: its reflection
direction needs a new kind of logical relation on denotations, which
we call \emph{approximant logical relations}. To evaluate this
denotational approach compared to operational ones, we prove the same
compiler correct in three operational styles and report on the size of
the proofs. The denotational end-to-end proof of correctness comes in
smaller than the proofs using abstract machines and reduction
semantics, but larger than the proof using clocked interpreters.  In
particular, the denotational approach yields elegant proofs for
compiler passes in which the program values remain at the same level
of abstraction, such as inlining, where equational reasoning suffices.
But the denotational proofs are more involved when program values are
translated to a lower level of abstraction, such as lowering closures
to their flat representation.
