Denotational semantics promises compositional, equational reasoning
about programs, and compiler correctness was among its first
applications, yet verified compilers today are almost entirely
operational. Denotational compiler proofs, on paper or mechanized,
have been either (1) limited to source languages that are terminating
or first-order, or (2) compile to an abstract machine that keeps
closures as primitive values and so avoids closure conversion. We use
a denotational semantics based on a graph model to prove a ten-pass
compiler correct, from a call-by-value $\lambda$-calculus with
iso-recursive types (which supports general recursion), through
closure conversion, to a typed assembly language.  The proof is
mechanized in Agda. Every intermediate language has a denotational
semantics, and most passes are proved by an equation between the
denotations of a program before and after the pass. The exception is
the step of closure conversion that lowers closures into code and
environment pairs: its reflection direction needs a new kind of
logical relation between two denotational semantics, which we name an
approximant logical relation. To evaluate this denotational approach
to the well-established operational approaches, we prove the same
compiler correct in three operational styles and report on the size of
the proofs. The denotational proof of the compiler totals 1958 lines,
between those for clocked interpreters (1653) and abstract machines
(2258) and well below reduction semantics (3207). The denotational
approach gives the smallest proofs of correctness for inlining,
hoisting and conversion to a control-flow graph, but the largest proof
for closure lowering.
