Denotational semantics promises compositional, equational reasoning
about programs, and compiler correctness was among its first
applications, yet most verified compilers today are proved correct
with respect to operational semantics. The denotational compiler
proofs in the literature either (1) are limited to source languages
that are first-order or strongly normalizing, (2) compile to target
languages that treat closures as primitive values and so avoid closure
conversion, or (3) prove only semantic preservation and not
reflection, which does not rule out a diverging program compiling to
one that terminates. We use a denotational semantics based on a graph
model to prove a compiler correct, from a call-by-value
$\lambda$-calculus with iso-recursive types (so it admits divergence),
through closure conversion, to a typed assembly language. The proof is
total: the compiler preserves and reflects divergence. We prove most
passes correct by an equation between the denotations before and after
the pass. The exception is the step of closure conversion that lowers
closures to pairs of code and environment: its reflection direction
needs a new kind of logical relation on denotations, which we
call \emph{approximant logical relations}. We then prove the same
compiler correct with Scott domains in place of the graph model, which
gives a second denotational proof of closure conversion that preserves
and reflects divergence. There, the lowering of closures needs a
logical relation defined by recursion on the stages of the domain. We
mechanize both proofs in Agda.

To compare these denotational approaches with operational ones, we
prove the same compiler correct three more times and compare the sizes
of the five proofs. Ranking the proofs from smallest to largest, not
counting language-independent infrastructure, gives the ordering (1)
clocked interpreters, (2) the graph model, (3) abstract machines, (4)
Scott domains, and (5) reduction semantics. The graph model yields
smaller proofs than Scott domains for every pass. For passes where
equational reasoning suffices, it tends to yield proofs that are
smaller or similar in size to the operational styles. For the one pass
where equational reasoning is insufficient, the lowering of closures,
the graph model yields a much larger proof than the operational ones.
