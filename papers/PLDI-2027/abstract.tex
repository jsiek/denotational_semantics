Denotational semantics promises compositional, equational reasoning
about programs, and compiler correctness was among its first
applications, yet verified compilers today are almost entirely
operational. The denotational compiler proofs in the literature either
(1) are limited to source languages that are first-order or strongly
normalizing, (2) compile to target languages that treat closures as
primitive values and so avoid closure conversion, or (3) prove only
semantic preservation and not reflection, which does not rule out a
diverging program compiling to one that terminates. We use a
denotational semantics based on a graph model to prove a compiler
correct, from a call-by-value $\lambda$-calculus with iso-recursive
types (so it supports general recursion and admits divergence),
through closure conversion, to a typed assembly language. The proof is
total: the compiler preserves and reflects divergence. We prove most
passes correct by an equation between the denotations of a program
before and after the pass. The exception is the step of closure
conversion that lowers closures to pairs of code and environment: its
reflection direction needs a new kind of logical relation on
denotations, which we call \emph{approximant logical relations}. We
then prove the same compiler correct with Scott domains in place of the
graph model, which gives a second denotational proof of closure
conversion that preserves and reflects divergence. There, the lowering
of closures needs a logical relation defined by recursion on the stages
of the domain. We mechanize both proofs in Agda.

To compare these denotational approaches with operational ones, we
prove the same compiler correct three more times and compare the sizes
of the five proofs. Ranking the proof from smallest to largest based
on the style of semantics, and not counting language-independent
infrastructure, yields (1) clocked interpreters, (2) graph-model, (3)
abstract machines, (4) Scott domains, and (4) reduction semantics. The
graph model enables smaller correctness proofs than the Scott domains
on all the passes and tends to be smaller than the operational proofs
on passes that do not change the representation of values, where
equational reasoning suffices. However, the graph model yields much
larger proofs than the operational ones for passes that change the
representation of values, such as the lowering of closures.
