Denotational semantics promises compositional, equational reasoning
about programs, and compiler correctness was among its first
applications, yet verified compilers today are almost entirely
operational. The denotational compiler proofs in the literature either
(1) are limited to source languages that are first-order or strongly
normalizing, (2) compile to target languages that treat closures as
primitive values and so avoid closure conversion, or (3) prove only
semantic preservation, which does not rule out a diverging program
compiling to one that terminates. We use a denotational
semantics based on a graph model to prove a compiler correct, from a
call-by-value $\lambda$-calculus with iso-recursive types (so it
supports general recursion and admits divergence), through closure
conversion, to a typed assembly language. The proof is total:
divergence is preserved and reflected. Every intermediate language has
a denotational semantics, and most passes are proved by an equation
between the denotations of a program before and after the pass. The
exception is the step of closure conversion that lowers closures into
code and environment pairs: its reflection direction needs a new kind
of logical relation on denotations, which we call \emph{approximant
logical relations}. The proof is mechanized in Agda.

To compare this denotational approach with operational ones, we prove
correctness of the same compiler in three operational styles and report
on the size of the proofs. Under a common accounting of the four
whole-compiler correctness proofs, the denotational development is
smaller than the proofs using abstract machines and reduction
semantics, but larger than the proof using clocked interpreters.  In
particular, the denotational approach yields
smaller proofs for compiler passes in which the program values remain
at the same level of abstraction, such as inlining, where equational
reasoning suffices.  But the denotational proofs are larger for
compiler passes that translate program values into a lower level of
abstraction, such as lowering closures to their flat representation.
