\section{Evaluation: Comparison with Operational Approaches}
\label{sec:evaluation}

To measure how concise the denotational proofs are (RQ2) and how the
denotational approach compares overall with operational ones (RQ4), we
proved the same compiler correct three more times, in three operational
styles. This section describes the four developments, compares the
sizes of their proofs pass by pass, and explains the differences.

\subsection{Setup}

All four developments prove the correctness of the same ten-pass
compiler (Section~\ref{sec:compiler}), from the same Agda definitions of
the languages and passes, and each is our best effort in its style.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Graph model.} The semantics of each language is the
  graph-model semantics of Appendix~\ref{app:languages}, and each pass
  is proved as in Section~\ref{sec:compiler-proof}.
\item \emph{Abstract machines and simulations}, in the style of
  CompCert \citep{Leroy:2009aa}. Each language has a small-step CEK
  machine, and each pass is proved by a forward simulation. The
  backward direction follows from forward simulation and the
  determinism of the machines.
\item \emph{Clocked interpreters and value relations}, in the style of
  CakeML \citep{Kumar:2014aa}. Each language has a total interpreter
  with a clock that ticks each time the interpreted program calls a
  function, and each pass is
  proved by relating the results of the two interpreters at the same
  clock.
\item \emph{Reduction semantics and simulations.} Each language has a
  reduction semantics, using substitution for function application,
  and each pass is proved by a simulation between reductions.
\end{itemize}
The three operational correctness theorems say that the compiled
program terminates with the same observation as the source, and that
one diverges exactly when the other does. Our adequacy results show
that the denotational correctness theorem is equivalent
(Section~\ref{sec:adequacy}).

We measure each proof by its lines of Agda, not counting comments,
blank lines, or imports. The counts include the semantics of the
languages, the proof of each pass, and the composition of the passes.
They exclude the Agda code that all four proofs use unchanged: the
intrinsically typed syntax of the languages and the functions that
implement the passes (921 lines). We report the language-independent infrastructure that
each style relies on, and the adequacy of the graph model, separately.

\paragraph{AI Use}

All of the Agda code of the four developments, including the adequacy
proofs, was written in 2026 by Claude Code (Opus 5.5 and Sonnet 5.5

of ISWIM, with proofs of soundness and adequacy with respect to their
operational semantics, together with several unfinished attempts to
prove closure conversion correct. The passes of the back end

operational proofs were adapted from the Agda development of \citet{Siek:2025ab}, which

the script that counts the lines of the proofs and assisted with the
literature search. The mechanized results do not depend on trusting
the AI: every development is checked by Agda in safe mode, with no
postulates, and we checked every theorem statement ourselves. We also
checked each citation and each claim about prior work against its

\subsection{Analysis}

Two factors seem to drive the sizes in Table~\ref{tab:loc}. The size of
a denotational proof tends to depend most on whether the pass preserves
the meaning of a program exactly. When it does, the proof is an
induction whose cases are congruences and a few laws of the graph
model (Section~\ref{sec:equational}), largely independent of how the
pass changes the way the program runs. The size of an operational
proof tends to depend instead on how closely the run of the target
follows the run of the source. On the denotational side, the correctness of nine
of the ten passes is an equation between the meaning of a program and
the meaning of its translation; only \pass{delay} is the exception. On
the operational side, the runs of the source and target correspond
closely for most passes, including \pass{delay}, but differ more for
\pass{simplify} and, to a lesser degree, \pass{globalize}. Combining
the two factors gives three groups of passes, which we discuss in turn.

\paragraph{When denotational correctness is an equation and the runs correspond closely, the interpreter proofs tend to be slightly smaller.}

For \pass{enclose}, \pass{optimize}, \pass{concretize}, \pass{remove
  complex operands}, \pass{lift locals}, \pass{explicate control}, and
\pass{select instructions}, both the denotational and the interpreter
proofs are short. The interpreter proofs are smaller by 1 to 28 lines,
except for \pass{explicate control}, where the two are about the same
size. The difference is not in the congruence cases, which take one
line per construct in both styles. In the graph model, a term denotes
a set of values and several operators place conditions on these sets:
strict closures, $\kw{let}$s, and writes require their sets to be
nonempty, and the binders require them to be consistent. The
denotational proofs must establish these conditions, whereas they hold
trivially for the single value that an interpreter computes. It must
also apply laws of the graph model that have premises, where an
interpreter simply runs: the $\beta$-law of Lemma~\ref{lem:beta}, and
commuting conversions that move a $\kw{let}$ or $\kw{case}$ past a
continuation. These costs are largest, relative to the interpreter
proofs, for \pass{lift locals} and \pass{select instructions}. Their
denotational proofs are about 1.4 and 1.6 times the size of the
interpreter proofs. In \pass{lift locals}, the continuation-passing
semantics of Imp requires a commuting conversion.  In \pass{select
  instructions}, the prologue of a function body in x86$\mu$ requires
two uses of Lemma~\ref{lem:beta}. The tendency concerns the
interpreters only. The reduction semantics, which works by
substitution, is often much larger even for these passes, as for
\pass{optimize} and \pass{lift locals}.

\paragraph{When denotational correctness is an equation but the runs differ more, the denotational proof tends to be smallest.}

\pass{simplify} is the clearest case, at 349 lines against 584 to 715.
It reduces programs under binders and removes calls, so the target
takes fewer steps than the source. Each operational proof must replay
those reductions as steps of the source program: on open terms in the
reduction semantics, or as a run-ahead of the source machine or
interpreter, with stuttering where the target does more. The
interpreter proof is the largest of the three, because the target
needs fewer clock ticks than the source, which breaks the usual
clock-for-clock argument. \pass{globalize} fits the pattern less
cleanly. Its interpreter proof is still clock for clock; the runs
differ only in where the code of a function lives, since in the target
it is a reference into a table of definitions. The interpreter proof
relates the two tables value by value, while the denotational proof
shows that a reference means what the code it replaced means.

\paragraph{When denotational correctness is not an equation, the operational proofs are much smaller.}

\pass{Delay} changes how closures are represented, so its correctness
is not an equation, yet the run of the target follows the run of the
source closely. Its denotational proof takes 524 lines of Agda,
against 35 to 139 lines in the three operational styles. Most of the
difference has three sources. First, the denotational proof needs two
directions, while a simulation proof gets the backward direction for
free from forward simulation and the determinism of the target. The
graph model has an analogue of determinism, the consistency of
denotations, and it does part of the job: if the source produces a
constant, the forward direction puts that constant in the target, and
consistency rules out any other. But the graph model represents
divergence as the empty set, the absence of evidence, and a forward
relation cannot carry absence. Showing that a diverging program
compiles to a diverging program is therefore a separate argument, and
that argument is reflection. We use different relations for the two
directions, the satisfaction relation for preservation and the
approximant logical relation for reflection, because a single relation
parameterized by the direction made the proof longer: on an earlier
version of the compiler, switching from one relation to two cut the
proof of \pass{delay} from 1,358 to 832 lines. Second, reflection
needs the finite lists, the batches of calls, and the lemma that
extracts them from a target call. Third, the $\beta$-law of
Lemma~\ref{lem:beta} holds only under side conditions of nonemptiness,
consistency, and continuity, whereas a machine does not have such side
conditions.

\paragraph{The graph-model semantics is about as small as the interpreters', but it needs more infrastructure.}

The graph-model semantics of the ten languages is 395 lines, slightly
more than the interpreters' 374. The machines need 575 lines and the
reduction semantics 965, which include the laws of substitution and
the machines of the first-order languages. The language-independent
infrastructure, which the totals exclude, differs more. The graph model
relies on 1,544 lines of it, mostly the graph model itself and its
operators, against 447 for the machines, 413 for the interpreters, and
1,191 for the reduction semantics. Adequacy, which connects the graph
model to the interpreters of the source and target languages, is a
further 1,529 lines (Section~\ref{sec:adequacy}).

\subsection{The Answers to RQ2 and RQ4}

The comparison supports the answer to RQ2. For the passes that keep
the representation of values, the equational proofs tend to be
concise: the smallest of the four styles for \pass{simplify} and
\pass{globalize}, about the same as the smallest for \pass{explicate
  control}, and within 30 lines of the smallest for the others, though
\pass{lift locals} and \pass{select instructions} are relatively the
furthest behind. The answer to RQ4 is mixed. Overall, the denotational
development ranks second, behind the clocked interpreters and ahead of
abstract machines and reduction semantics. Its advantage on the passes
proved by equational reasoning such as \pass{simplify} is neutralized
by \pass{delay}, the one pass that is not an equation.

\subsection{Threats to Validity}

We measure the size of the proofs, which is only a proxy for the
effort of writing them. Lines of Agda depend on layout; lexical tokens
and non-whitespace characters do not, but they are proxies too. The
three measures agree on the ranking: each style averages 20 to 21
tokens per line, and the graph model's total is 1.12 times the
interpreters' by tokens, against 1.14 times by lines. Size also says
little about difficulty, which depends heavily on whether a proof has
precedent in the literature or must be invented. The reflection proof
for \pass{delay} took us far more effort than its size suggests,
because no denotational proof of its kind existed; with the
approximant logical relation in hand, a similar proof should be much
easier to write. Each development is our best effort, but experience
gained in one style carries over to the next.

The comparison covers one compiler, and the totals depend on its
selection of passes. The denotational proofs tend to be smaller for
optimizations such as \pass{simplify} and much larger for passes that
change the representation of values such as \pass{delay}, so a
compiler with more of one kind or the other could rank the styles
differently. The sizes also depend on choices of semantics, for
example the continuation-passing semantics of Imp, which makes
\pass{explicate control} an equation, and on proof engineering that
could still shrink any of the four developments.

The sizes also depend on the proof assistant. Agda offers little proof
automation apart from normalization: it unfolds definitions
aggressively during type checking, which probably favours the
interpreters, whose results Agda can compute directly. In Rocq or Lean,
the sizes would depend heavily on the tactics used, and the relative
sizes of the four styles could be quite different. Finally, the
developments share infrastructure unevenly, so we report it separately
rather than in the totals.
