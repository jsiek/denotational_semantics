\section{Evaluation: Comparison with Operational Approaches}
\label{sec:evaluation}

To measure how concise the denotational proofs are (RQ2) and how the
denotational approach compares overall with operational ones (RQ4), we
proved the same compiler correct three more times, in three operational
styles. This section describes the four developments, compares the
sizes of their proofs pass by pass, and explains the differences.

\subsection{Setup}

All four developments prove the correctness of the same ten-pass
compiler (Section~\ref{sec:compiler}), from the same Agda definitions of
the languages and passes, and each is our best effort in its style.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Graph model.} The semantics of each language is the
  graph-model semantics of Appendix~\ref{app:languages}, and each pass
  is proved as in Section~\ref{sec:compiler-proof}.
\item \emph{Abstract machines and simulations}, in the style of
  CompCert \citep{Leroy:2009aa}. Each language has a small-step CEK
  machine, and each pass is proved by a forward simulation. The
  backward direction follows from forward simulation and the
  determinism of the machines.
\item \emph{Clocked interpreters and value relations}, in the style of
  CakeML \citep{Kumar:2014aa}. Each language has a total interpreter
  with a clock that ticks each time the interpreted program calls a
  function, and each pass is
  proved by relating the results of the two interpreters at the same
  clock.
\item \emph{Reduction semantics and simulations.} Each language has a
  reduction semantics, using substitution for function application,
  and each pass is proved by a simulation between reductions.
\end{itemize}
The three operational correctness theorems say that the compiled
program terminates with the same observation as the source, and that
one diverges exactly when the other does. Our adequacy results show
that the denotational correctness theorem is equivalent
(Section~\ref{sec:adequacy}).

We measure each proof by its lines of Agda, not counting comments,
blank lines, or imports. The counts include the semantics of the
languages, the proof of each pass, and the composition of the passes.
They exclude the Agda code that all four proofs use unchanged: the
intrinsically typed syntax of the languages and the functions that
implement the passes (921 lines). We report the language-independent infrastructure that
each style relies on, and the adequacy of the graph model, separately.

\subsection{Analysis}

\paragraph{The graph model tends to be smallest, or close to it, when a pass is proved by equational reasoning.}

For the nine passes proved by an equation, the denotational proof is
the smallest for \pass{simplify} and \pass{globalize}, about the same
size as the smallest for \pass{explicate control}, and at most 28
lines larger than the smallest for the other six. Two factors seem to
drive these sizes. The size of a denotational proof tends to depend
most on whether the pass preserves the meaning of a program exactly.
When it does, the proof is an induction whose cases are congruences
and a few laws of the graph model (Section~\ref{sec:equational}),
largely independent of how the pass changes the way the program runs.
The size of an operational proof tends to depend instead on how
closely the run of the target follows the run of the source.

When the two runs correspond closely, as for most of the passes, the
interpreter proofs tend to be short as well, and slightly smaller than
the denotational ones. The difference is not in the congruence cases,
which take one line per construct in both styles. A denotation is a
set of values, so the graph model must prove side conditions that hold
trivially for the single value that an interpreter computes: that the
sets involved are nonempty, for strict closures, $\kw{let}$s, and
writes, and that they are consistent, for the binders. It must also
apply $\beta$ and related laws, with their premises, where an
interpreter simply runs. These costs are largest, in relative terms,
for \pass{lift locals} and \pass{select instructions}, whose
denotational proofs are about 1.4 and 1.6 times the interpreter
proofs: the continuation-passing semantics of Imp puts a commuting
conversion into \pass{lift locals}, and the prologue of a function body
in x86$\mu$ takes two $\beta$-steps. The tendency also does not extend
to every operational style: the reduction semantics, which works by
substitution, is often much larger even for these passes, as for
\pass{optimize} and \pass{lift locals}.

When the two runs differ more, the operational proofs tend to grow,
and the graph model comes out ahead. \pass{simplify} is the clearest
case, at 349 lines against 584 to 715. It reduces programs under
binders and removes calls, so the target takes fewer steps than the
source. Each operational proof must replay those reductions as steps
of the source program: on open terms in the reduction semantics, or as
a run-ahead of the source machine or interpreter, with stuttering
where the target does more. The interpreter proof is the largest of
the three, because the target needs fewer clock ticks than the source,
which breaks the usual clock-for-clock argument. \pass{globalize} fits
the pattern less cleanly. Its interpreter proof is still clock for
clock; the runs differ only in where the code of a function lives,
since in the target it is a reference into a table of definitions.
The interpreter proof relates the two tables value by value, while the
denotational proof shows that a reference means what the code it
replaced means.

\paragraph{\pass{Delay} is the pass whose denotational proof costs the most.}

\pass{Delay} shows the opposite tendency. It is not an equation,
because it changes how closures are represented, yet the run of the
target follows the run of the source closely. The denotational proof of \pass{delay} takes 524 lines of Agda,
against 35 to 139 lines for the same pass in the three operational
styles. Most of the difference has three sources. First, the
denotational proof needs two directions, while a simulation proof gets
the backward direction for free from forward simulation and the
determinism of the target. The graph model has an analogue of
determinism, the consistency of denotations, and it does part of the
job: if the source produces a constant, the forward direction puts
that constant in the target, and consistency rules out any other. But
the graph model represents divergence as the empty set, the absence of
evidence, and a forward relation cannot carry absence. Showing that a
diverging program compiles to a diverging program is therefore a
separate argument, and that argument is reflection. We use different
relations for the two directions, the satisfaction relation for
preservation and the approximant logical relation for reflection,
because a single relation parameterized by the direction made the
proof longer: on an earlier version of the compiler, switching from
one relation to two cut the proof of \pass{delay} from 1,358 to 832
lines. Second, reflection needs the finite lists, the batches of calls,
and the lemma that extracts them from a target call. Third, the
$\beta$-law of the graph model holds only under side conditions of
nonemptiness and continuity, which a machine gets for free by taking a
step.

\paragraph{The semantics and the infrastructure}

The graph-model semantics of the ten languages is 395 lines, slightly more than the interpreters' 374. The machines need 575 lines and the
reduction semantics 965, which include the laws of substitution and
the machines of the first-order languages. The infrastructure that the
counts exclude goes the other way. The graph model relies on 1,544
lines of language-independent infrastructure, mostly the graph model
itself and its operators, against 447 for the machines, 413 for the
interpreters, and 1,191 for the reduction semantics. Adequacy, which
connects the graph model to the interpreters of the source and target
languages, is a further 1,529 lines (Section~\ref{sec:adequacy}).

\subsection{The Answers to RQ2 and RQ4}

The comparison supports the answer to RQ2. For the passes that keep
the representation of values, the equational proofs tend to be
concise: the smallest of the four styles for \pass{simplify} and
\pass{globalize}, about the same as the smallest for \pass{explicate
control}, and within 30 lines of the smallest for the others, though
\pass{lift locals} and \pass{select instructions} are relatively the
furthest behind. The answer to RQ4 is mixed. Overall, the denotational
development ranks second, behind the clocked interpreters and ahead of
abstract machines and reduction semantics. Its advantage on the passes
that keep the representation of values is outweighed by \pass{delay},
the one pass that changes it.

\subsection{Threats to Validity}

Lines of Agda are only a proxy for proof effort. Counting lexical
tokens instead, which is insensitive to layout, gives the same ranking:
each style averages 20 to 21 tokens per line, and the graph model's
total is 1.12 times the interpreters' by tokens, against 1.14 times by
lines. Counting non-whitespace characters also gives the same ranking. Each development is
our best effort, but experience gained in one style carries over to
the next. The comparison
covers one compiler, and the sizes depend on choices of semantics, for
example the continuation-passing semantics of Imp, which makes
\pass{explicate control} an equation, and on proof engineering that
could still shrink any of the four developments. Finally, the
developments share infrastructure unevenly, so we report it
separately rather than in the totals.
