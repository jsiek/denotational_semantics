\section{Evaluation: Comparison with Other Semantics}
\label{sec:evaluation}

To measure how concise the denotational proofs are (RQ2), how the
denotational proofs compare with operational ones (RQ4), and how the
graph model compares with Scott domains (RQ5), we proved the same
compiler correct three more times using several styles of operational
semantics. This section summarizes the five developments, compares the
sizes of their proofs pass by pass, and explains the differences.

\subsection{Setup}

All five developments prove the correctness of the same ten-pass
compiler (Section~\ref{sec:compiler}), from the same Agda definitions of
the languages and passes, and each is our best effort in its style.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Graph model.} The semantics of each language is the
  graph-model semantics of Appendix~\ref{app:languages}, and we prove
  each pass as in Section~\ref{sec:compiler-proof}.
\item \emph{Scott domains.} Meanings are continuous functions into a
  universal domain of tagged values (Appendix~\ref{app:scott}), built
  as a bilimit with the domain theory of TypeTopology
  \citep{Escardo:TypeTopology}.
\item \emph{Abstract machines and simulations}, in the style of
  CompCert \citep{Leroy:2009aa}. Each language has a small-step CEK
  machine (Appendix~\ref{app:machines}), and we prove each pass by a
  forward simulation. The backward direction follows from forward
  simulation and determinism.
\item \emph{Clocked interpreters and value relations}, in the style of
  CakeML \citep{Kumar:2014aa}. (Clocked interpreters go back to
  \citet{Boyer:1984aa}.) Each language has a total interpreter with a
  clock that ticks each time the interpreted program calls a function
  (Appendix~\ref{app:interp}),
  and we prove each pass by showing that if one interpreter returns a
  result at some clock, the other returns a related result at some
  clock.
\item \emph{Reduction semantics and simulations.} Each language has a
  reduction semantics, using substitution for function application
  (Appendix~\ref{app:reduction}),
  and we prove each pass by a simulation between reductions.
\end{itemize}
Appendix~\ref{app:other} states each style's theorem, with its
semantics of \iswim\ and x86$\mu$.

\paragraph{The correctness theorems and their proofs}

Each development proves the compiler correct with respect to its own
semantics, for closed programs of integer type. The denotational
theorems state that a program and its compilation produce the same
integer and that one has a meaning exactly when the other does; the
operational theorems state that both terminate with the same integer
or both diverge, for three different operational semantics. All five
are whole-program theorems, which say nothing about compiled code
linked with other code. We do not regard the operational or
denotational formulation of correctness as more fundamental than the
other. \citet{Scott:1971ab} proposed a mathematical semantics as ``the
standard against which to judge an implementation'', while
\citet{Plotkin:1977oc} judged denotational semantics by their
closeness to an operational one. Our adequacy proof
(Section~\ref{sec:adequacy}) makes the choice immaterial. For programs
of integer type, the graph-model theorem and the interpreter theorem
are equivalent. We therefore count each development up to its own
theorem and report adequacy, 1,529 lines, separately.

We measure each proof by its lines of Agda, not counting comments,
blank lines, or imports. The counts include the semantics of the
languages, the proof of each pass, and the composition of the passes.
They exclude the code that all five proofs use unchanged: the
intrinsically typed syntax of the languages and the functions that
implement the passes (921 lines). We report separately the language-independent infrastructure of each style.

\paragraph{AI Use}

All of the Agda code of the five developments was written by Claude
Code (Opus 5.5 and Sonnet 5.5) under our direction. Claude started
from our earlier Agda mechanizations of graph models of the
$\lambda$-calculus and ISWIM, which included adequacy proofs and a
partial proof of correctness for closure conversion. The results do
not depend on trusting the AI. Agda checks every development in safe
mode, with no postulates. However, the Scott-domain theorems take
three standard axioms as hypotheses because it depends on
TypeTopology. The authors checked all the definitions and every
theorem statement.

\subsection{Analysis}

Two factors seem to drive the sizes in Table~\ref{tab:loc}. The size
of the denotational proofs tend to depend most on whether the pass
preserves the meaning of a program exactly. When it does, the proof is
an induction whose cases are congruences and a few laws of the model
(Section~\ref{sec:equational}), largely independent of how the pass
changes the way the program runs. The size of an operational proof
tends to depend instead on how closely the run of the target follows
the run of the source. On the denotational side, the correctness of
nine of the ten passes is an equation between the meaning of a program
and the meaning of its translation; only \pass{delay} is the
exception. On the operational side, the runs of the source and target
correspond closely for most passes, including \pass{delay}, but differ
more for \pass{simplify} and, to a lesser degree,
\pass{globalize}. Combining the two factors gives three groups of
passes, which we discuss in turn.

\paragraph{Equal meanings, similar runs: the interpreter proofs tend to be smaller.}

For \pass{enclose}, \pass{optimize}, \pass{concretize}, \pass{remove
  complex operands}, \pass{lift locals}, \pass{explicate control}, and
\pass{select instructions}, both the graph-model and the interpreter
proofs are short. The interpreter proofs are smaller by 1 to 28 lines,
except for \pass{explicate control}, where the two are about the same
size. The Scott-domain proofs of these passes are 1.2 to 2.8 times the
interpreter proofs, for the reasons given at the end of this
section. The congruence cases take one line per construct in both
styles. The difference lies in the obligations of the graph model.
Because a term denotes a set of values, some operators require their
sets to be nonempty or consistent, and some laws have premises, such as
the $\beta$-law of Lemma~\ref{lem:beta} and the commuting conversions
that move a $\kw{let}$ or $\kw{case}$ past a continuation. An
interpreter computes a single value, so these obligations do not arise.
They cost the most for \pass{lift locals}, whose continuation-passing
semantics needs a commuting conversion, and for \pass{select
  instructions}, whose function prologue needs two uses of
Lemma~\ref{lem:beta}; these graph-model proofs are about 1.4 and 1.6
times the interpreter proofs. The tendency concerns the interpreters
only. The proofs in the reduction semantics, which works by
substitution, are often several times larger even for these passes, as for \pass{optimize} and
\pass{lift locals}.

\paragraph{Equal meanings, different runs: the denotational proofs tend to be smallest.}

\pass{simplify} is the clearest case, at 349 lines in the graph model
and 411 with Scott domains, against 508 to 697 for the operational
proofs.  This pass reduces expression under binders and inlines calls,
so the target takes fewer steps than the source. Each operational
proof must replay those reductions as steps of the source program: on
open terms in the reduction semantics, or as a run-ahead of the source
machine or interpreter, with stuttering where the target does
more. For the interpreters, the target needs fewer clock ticks than
the source, which breaks the usual clock-for-clock argument. The
\pass{globalize} pass fits the pattern less cleanly, at 115 lines in
the graph model and 147 with Scott domains, against 175 to 213 for the
operational proofs. The interpreter proof is clock for clock; the runs
differ only in where the code of a function lives, because in the
target it is a reference into a table of definitions. The interpreter
proof relates the two tables value by value, while the denotational
proof shows that the meaning of a reference equals the meaning of the
code it replaces.

\paragraph{Changed representations: the operational proofs are several times smaller.}

\pass{Delay} changes the representation of closures, so its correctness
is not an equation, yet the run of the target follows the run of the
source closely. Its denotational proofs take 450 lines of Agda in the
graph model and 723 with Scott domains, against 35 to 126 lines in the
three operational styles. For the graph model, most of the difference
has three sources. First, the denotational proof needs two
directions, while a simulation proof gets the backward direction for
free from forward simulation and the determinism of the target. The
graph model has an analogue of determinism, the consistency of
denotations, and it does part of the job. If the source produces a
constant, the forward direction puts that constant in the target, and
consistency rules out any other. But the graph model represents
divergence as the empty set, the absence of evidence, and a forward
relation cannot carry absence. Showing that a diverging program
compiles to a diverging program is therefore a separate argument, and
that argument is reflection. Second, reflection
needs the finite lists, the batches of calls, and the lemma that
extracts them from a target call. Third, the $\beta$-law of
Lemma~\ref{lem:beta} holds only under side conditions of nonemptiness,
consistency, and continuity, whereas a machine does not have such side
conditions. The Scott-domain proof shares the first source, and its
stage relations replace the other two with the costs discussed
next.

\paragraph{The graph model is smaller than Scott domains.}

The two denotational proofs have the same structure, yet the
Scott-domain proof is 1.4 times the graph model (1.3 by tokens),
larger in every row but the semantics: 1.2 to 1.8 times for the
equational passes, and 723 lines against 450 for \pass{delay}. Three
properties of Scott domains explain much of the difference. First,
Scott values are infinite, so the relation for \pass{delay} is built
by stages (Section~\ref{sec:scott-delay}), and showing that each stage
is a proposition, downward closed, admissible, and preserved by the
maps between stages takes 174 of the 723 lines of its proof. Second,
relating calls across stages takes 299 lines, the largest
share. Third, the universal domain is a sum of tagged values. The
primitives and tagged values take 71 lines, and the 135 lines of
shared lemmas are laws of strictness and definedness, which replace
nonemptiness; in exchange, the proof needs no consistency. The
graph-model relations recurse on finite elements, and neither of the
first two costs arises. The infrastructure widens the gap further
(Table~\ref{tab:infra}). The rest of the gap is a property of Agda:
its \texttt{with}-abstraction is slow when a value of the domain is in
scope, so six places in five passes use auxiliary lemmas, records, or
an opaque definition instead.


\subsection{Threats to Validity}

We measure the size of the proofs, which is only a proxy for the
effort of writing them. Lines of Agda depend on layout; lexical tokens
and non-whitespace characters do not, but they are proxies too. The
three measures agree on the ranking. For example, by tokens the graph model's
total is 1.22 times the interpreters', against 1.20 times by
lines. Size also says little about difficulty, which depends on
whether a proof has precedent in the literature or its authors must
invent it. Because experience carries over from one development to the
next, we gave every development a final cleanup pass under the same
rules (shorten and clarify the proofs but change no theorem statement
and move nothing into the uncounted infrastructure). The cleanup pass
did not change the order of the four other proofs; the Scott-domain proof,
written last, shrank from 3,457 to 2,485 lines, which moved it ahead
of reduction semantics.

The comparison covers one compiler, and the totals depend on its
selection of passes. The denotational proofs tend to be smaller for
optimizations such as \pass{simplify} and several times larger for
passes that change the representation of values such as \pass{delay},
so a compiler with more of one kind or the other could rank the styles
differently. The sizes also depend on choices of semantics, for
example the continuation-passing semantics of Imp, which makes
\pass{explicate control} an equation, and on proof engineering that
could still shrink any of the five developments.  Like our graph
model, the Scott-domain semantics uses one universal domain of tagged
values, as \citet{Milner:1978kh} and \citet{MacQueen:1986aa} did. A
domain for each type, more usual for typed languages
\citep{Benton:2009ab,Dockins:2014aa}, would avoid the case analysis on
tags, a tenth of the Scott-domain proof of \pass{delay}, but would
need a domain equation for each recursive type.

The sizes also depend on the proof assistant. Agda's main automation
is normalization, which probably favors the interpreters, whose
results it computes directly; with the tactics of Rocq or Lean, the
relative sizes of the five styles could differ.
