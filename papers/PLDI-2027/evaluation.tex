\section{Evaluation: Comparison with Other Proof Styles}
\label{sec:evaluation}

To measure how concise the denotational proofs are (RQ2), how the
graph-model proof compares with operational ones (RQ4), and how it
compares with Scott domains (RQ5), we
proved the same compiler correct four more times: once in a second denotational style, with Scott domains, and three times in operational styles. This section describes the five developments, compares the
sizes of their proofs pass by pass, and explains the differences.

\subsection{Setup}

All five developments prove the correctness of the same ten-pass
compiler (Section~\ref{sec:compiler}), from the same Agda definitions of
the languages and passes, and each is our best effort in its style.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Graph model.} The semantics of each language is the
  graph-model semantics of Appendix~\ref{app:languages}, and we prove each pass as in Section~\ref{sec:compiler-proof}.
\item \emph{Scott domains.} Meanings are continuous functions into a
  universal domain of tagged values (Appendix~\ref{app:scott}), built as
  a bilimit with the domain theory of TypeTopology
  \citep{Escardo:TypeTopology}.
\item \emph{Abstract machines and simulations}, in the style of
  CompCert \citep{Leroy:2009aa}. Each language has a small-step CEK
  machine, and we prove each pass by a forward simulation. The
  backward direction follows from forward simulation and the
  determinism of the machines.
\item \emph{Clocked interpreters and value relations}, in the style of
  CakeML \citep{Kumar:2014aa}, a technique that goes back to the clocked
  evaluator of \citet{Boyer:1984aa}. Each language has a total interpreter
  with a clock that ticks each time the interpreted program calls a
  function, and we prove each pass by relating the two interpreters' results at the same clock.
\item \emph{Reduction semantics and simulations.} Each language has a
  reduction semantics, using substitution for function application,
  and we prove each pass by a simulation between reductions.
\end{itemize}
Appendix~\ref{app:other} states each style's theorem, with its
semantics of \iswim\ and x86$\mu$.

\paragraph{What each theorem states}

Each development proves the compiler correct with respect to its own semantics, for closed programs of integer type. The denotational theorems state that a program and its compilation produce the same integer and that one has a meaning exactly when the other does; the operational theorems state that both terminate with the same integer or both diverge, for three different operational semantics. (The operational developments also prove that the observations agree at every type.) All five are whole-program theorems: they say nothing about compiled code linked with other code. We do not regard either kind of statement as the more
fundamental: \citet{Scott:1971ab} proposed a mathematical semantics as
``the standard against which to judge an implementation'', while
\citet{Plotkin:1977oc} judged denotational semantics by their closeness
to an operational one. Adequacy (Section~\ref{sec:adequacy}) makes the
choice immaterial for our compiler: for programs of integer type, it
makes the graph-model theorem and the interpreter theorem equivalent,
and an alternative proof does the same for the abstract machines. We
therefore count each development up to its own theorem and report
adequacy, 1,529 lines, separately.

We measure each proof by its lines of Agda, not counting comments,
blank lines, or imports. The counts include the semantics of the
languages, the proof of each pass, and the composition of the passes.
They exclude the code that all five proofs use unchanged: the
intrinsically typed syntax of the languages and the functions that
implement the passes (921 lines). We report separately the language-independent infrastructure of each style.

\subsection{Analysis}

Two factors seem to drive the sizes in Table~\ref{tab:loc}. The size of
a denotational proof tends to depend most on whether the pass preserves
the meaning of a program exactly. When it does, the proof is an
induction whose cases are congruences and a few laws of the graph
model (Section~\ref{sec:equational}), largely independent of how the
pass changes the way the program runs. The size of an operational
proof tends to depend instead on how closely the run of the target
follows the run of the source. On the denotational side, the correctness of nine
of the ten passes is an equation between the meaning of a program and
the meaning of its translation; only \pass{delay} is the exception. On
the operational side, the runs of the source and target correspond
closely for most passes, including \pass{delay}, but differ more for
\pass{simplify} and, to a lesser degree, \pass{globalize}. Combining
the two factors gives three groups of passes, which we discuss in turn.

\paragraph{Equal meanings, similar runs: the interpreter proofs tend to be slightly smaller.}

For \pass{enclose}, \pass{optimize}, \pass{concretize}, \pass{remove
  complex operands}, \pass{lift locals}, \pass{explicate control}, and
\pass{select instructions}, both the denotational and the interpreter
proofs are short. The interpreter proofs are smaller by 1 to 28 lines,
except for \pass{explicate control}, where the two are about the same
size. The difference is not in the congruence cases, which take one
line per construct in both styles. In the graph model, a term denotes
a set of values and several operators place conditions on these sets:
strict closures, $\kw{let}$s, and writes require their sets to be
nonempty, and the binders require them to be consistent. The
denotational proofs must establish these conditions, whereas they hold
trivially for the single value that an interpreter computes. It must
also apply laws of the graph model that have premises, where an
interpreter simply runs: the $\beta$-law of Lemma~\ref{lem:beta}, and
commuting conversions that move a $\kw{let}$ or $\kw{case}$ past a
continuation. These costs are largest, relative to the interpreter
proofs, for \pass{lift locals} and \pass{select instructions}. Their
denotational proofs are about 1.4 and 1.6 times the size of the
interpreter proofs. In \pass{lift locals}, the continuation-passing
semantics of Imp requires a commuting conversion.  In \pass{select
  instructions}, the prologue of a function body in x86$\mu$ requires
two uses of Lemma~\ref{lem:beta}. The tendency concerns the
interpreters only. The reduction semantics, which works by
substitution, is often much larger even for these passes, as for
\pass{optimize} and \pass{lift locals}.

\paragraph{Equal meanings, different runs: the denotational proof tends to be smallest.}

\pass{simplify} is the clearest case, at 349 lines against 508 to 697.
It reduces programs under binders and removes calls, so the target
takes fewer steps than the source. Each operational proof must replay
those reductions as steps of the source program: on open terms in the
reduction semantics, or as a run-ahead of the source machine or
interpreter, with stuttering where the target does more. For the
interpreters, the target needs fewer clock ticks than the source,
which breaks the usual clock-for-clock argument. \pass{globalize} fits the pattern less
cleanly. Its interpreter proof is still clock for clock; the runs
differ only in where the code of a function lives, since in the target
it is a reference into a table of definitions. The interpreter proof
relates the two tables value by value, while the denotational proof
shows that a reference means what the code it replaced means.

\paragraph{Changed representations: the operational proofs are much smaller.}

\pass{Delay} changes the representation of closures, so its correctness
is not an equation, yet the run of the target follows the run of the
source closely. Its denotational proof takes 450 lines of Agda,
against 35 to 126 lines in the three operational styles. Most of the
difference has three sources. First, the denotational proof needs two
directions, while a simulation proof gets the backward direction for
free from forward simulation and the determinism of the target. The
graph model has an analogue of determinism, the consistency of
denotations, and it does part of the job: if the source produces a
constant, the forward direction puts that constant in the target, and
consistency rules out any other. But the graph model represents
divergence as the empty set, the absence of evidence, and a forward
relation cannot carry absence. Showing that a diverging program
compiles to a diverging program is therefore a separate argument, and
that argument is reflection. Second, reflection
needs the finite lists, the batches of calls, and the lemma that
extracts them from a target call. Third, the $\beta$-law of
Lemma~\ref{lem:beta} holds only under side conditions of nonemptiness,
consistency, and continuity, whereas a machine does not have such side
conditions.

\paragraph{The graph model is smaller than Scott domains.}

The two denotational proofs have the same structure, yet the
Scott-domain proof is 1.4 times the graph model (1.3 by tokens),
larger in every row but the semantics: 1.2 to 1.8 times for the
equational passes, and 723 lines against 450 for \pass{delay}. Three
properties of Scott domains explain much of the difference. First, a
Scott value is infinite, so the relation for \pass{delay} cannot recurse
on values. We built it stage by stage along the bilimit, as
\citet{Reynolds:1974aa} did, with one relation, parameterized by the
direction, for both halves. The proof must show that each stage is a proposition, downward closed, admissible, and preserved by the maps
between stages: 174 of the 838 lines of the proof of \pass{delay}.
Second, the method of \citet{Pitts:1996aa} does not apply directly,
because \pass{delay} turns a function into a pair of code and
environment, so the closure clause looks three levels into the target
domain but only one into the source. A call of a pair seen at stage
$m + 2$ shows its result only at stage $m$, and relating calls across
stages takes 299 lines, the largest share. Third, the universal domain
is a sum of tagged values: the primitives and tagged values take 71
lines, and the 135 lines of shared lemmas are laws of strictness and
definedness, which replace nonemptiness; in exchange, the proof needs no consistency. The graph-model relations recurse on finite elements, and neither of the first two costs arises. The infrastructure widens the gap further (Table~\ref{tab:infra}). The rest of the gap is a property of
Agda: its \texttt{with}-abstraction is very slow when a value of the
domain is in scope, so six places in five passes use auxiliary lemmas,
records, or an opaque definition instead.


\subsection{What the Comparison Shows}

The rows for the passes support a conclusion that does not depend on
how the totals are counted. Where a pass keeps the representation of
values, the graph-model proof is the smallest of the five for
\pass{simplify}, \pass{globalize}, and \pass{explicate control}, and
within 30 lines of the smallest for the others (RQ2). Where a pass
changes that representation, as \pass{delay} does, the operational
proofs are several times smaller. The totals, by contrast, depend on
the accounting. Counting each proof against its own semantics, the
graph model ranks second, and with the language-independent
infrastructure it ranks third (RQ4). Under both accountings, the graph
model gives a smaller proof than Scott domains (RQ5). All five
developments are ours, written with the same AI assistant, so the
totals measure these developments rather than the inherent difficulty
of each style.

\subsection{Threats to Validity}

We measure the size of the proofs, which is only a proxy for the
effort of writing them. Lines of Agda depend on layout; lexical tokens
and non-whitespace characters do not, but they are proxies too. The
three measures agree on the ranking: each style averages about 21 tokens per line, and the graph model's total is 1.22 times the interpreters' by tokens, against 1.20 times by lines. Size also says
little about difficulty, which depends heavily on whether a proof has precedent in the literature or its authors must invent it. The reflection proof
for \pass{delay} took us far more effort than its size suggests,
because no denotational proof of its kind existed; with the
approximant logical relation in hand, a similar proof should be much
easier to write. Because experience carries over from one development to the next, we
gave every development a final cleanup pass under the same rules
(shorten and clarify, change no theorem statement, move nothing into
the uncounted infrastructure). It did not change the ranking; the
Scott-domain proof, written last, shrank the most, from 3,457 to 2,485
lines.

The comparison covers one compiler, and the totals depend on its
selection of passes. The denotational proofs tend to be smaller for
optimizations such as \pass{simplify} and much larger for passes that
change the representation of values such as \pass{delay}, so a
compiler with more of one kind or the other could rank the styles
differently. The sizes also depend on choices of semantics, for
example the continuation-passing semantics of Imp, which makes
\pass{explicate control} an equation, and on proof engineering that
could still shrink any of the five developments.
Like our graph model, the Scott-domain semantics uses one universal
domain of tagged values, as \citet{Milner:1978kh} and
\citet{MacQueen:1986aa} did. A domain for each type, more usual for
typed languages \citep{Benton:2009ab,Dockins:2014aa}, would avoid the
case analysis on tags, a twelfth of the Scott-domain proof of
\pass{delay}, but would need a domain equation for each recursive type
and would leave the stage-indexed relations in place.

The sizes also depend on the proof assistant. Agda offers little proof
automation apart from normalization: it unfolds definitions
aggressively during type checking, which probably favors the interpreters, whose results Agda can compute directly. In Rocq or Lean,
the sizes would depend heavily on the tactics used, and the relative sizes of the five styles could be quite different.
